Research

We connect single-cell technology development with questions about cell identity, regulatory evolution and responses to persistent stress. We also study the molecular basis of immune recognition, with a focus on therapeutic protein immunogenicity.

Single-cell technologies

We develop single-cell RNA-seq, ATAC-seq and CUT&Tag technologies to measure transcriptional and epigenetic variation in complex tissues. Technology development is driven by biological questions, with an emphasis on scalable profiling and measurements that help resolve cell states and their regulatory programs.

Comparative cell atlases

We build single-cell multi-omics atlases of embryonic and adult tissues across species. These maps let us compare cell identities, developmental trajectories and regulatory programs, and ask which features are conserved and which are species-specific. The aim is to understand how cellular diversity and gene regulation have changed over evolution.

Enhancer evolution

We ask how changes in enhancer sequence preserve or alter regulatory function across species. We combine comparative epigenomics with sequence-to-function approaches, including ChromBPNet and CREsted, to develop hypotheses about regulatory sequence evolution. Massively parallel reporter assays (MPRA) and other functional experiments test these hypotheses in relevant cellular contexts.

Chronic stress and the epigenome

We investigate how persistent physiological stress remodels the epigenome across cell types and tissues. Our interests span metabolic, inflammatory and other chronic challenges, with obesity, hypertension and sleep deprivation among the contexts we examine. We ask how stress-associated regulatory changes affect tissue function, whether they persist, and how they relate to disease susceptibility, including tumor development.

Immune recognition and protein immunogenicity

We study how MHC class I molecules present peptides and how T-cell receptors recognize these peptide–MHC complexes. By investigating the rules of MHC-I–peptide–TCR interactions, we aim to understand unwanted T-cell responses to exogenous proteins and inform strategies for reducing their immunogenicity in therapeutic use.
